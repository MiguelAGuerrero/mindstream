# Primary sources shrink Mindstream's novelty claims

Reading the primary sources in full overturns two of the earlier report's "no comparable feature found" claims outright and narrows a third, while leaving the rest of the comparison standing. A manual push-and-return focus stack exists as a small genre of developer tools (yakstack, memo, rabbit_hole, stackly, todostack, sigi), Intend's "Now Page" already starts blank and asks "What now?", and Ink & Switch's Upwelling prototype already freezes merged text and admits change only through separate titled drafts merged as a batch; the Muse prototype also opened a blank nested board years before ThinkPad's blank sub-pad. The nearest neighbours are otherwise unchanged: Workflowy and Org-mode for drilling, Ulysses and Scrivener for a revision phase, Llama Life, Amazing Marvin, Sunsama and Super Productivity for single-task execution, Stoic, Day One and CBT thought records for ordered prompts, and VS Code, Android, FDC3 and JupyterLab for the planned shell. What no source read supplies is narrower than before: a blank page bound to one span of a parent text, a condensed result that replaces that span with the working-out archived, revision limited to a fixed set of moves after the freeze, a takeaway prompted at pop and filed on the parent, and a whole stack parked with a "waiting on" reason. Mindstream should therefore describe itself as a specific combination and a set of rules about the order of thinking, never as the first tool to freeze a draft, stack a focus or start from a blank prompt. The planned shell is confirmed as a textbook messaging design (Routing Slip, Request-Reply, Return Address, Correlation Identifier), which brings both a ready vocabulary and the pattern authors' stated limit: a slip is fixed up-front and linear. The check was partial: Workflowy's help articles, Tana Outliner's docs, Obsidian's user help, the Org manual, Blitzit and Llama Life's help centre stayed unreadable, and the whole workflows strand plus Android, Apple, Raycast, Linear, Jira, Rust and most task apps were not rechecked.

## ThinkPad: Upwelling froze drafts first, and Muse opened the blank page

### Drilling and the blank sub-pad

Drilling into a node with a breadcrumb remains a mature pattern, but the evidence has shifted. The earlier report cited Workflowy's help pages for breadcrumbs that collapse into an ellipsis ([Workflowy help](https://workflowy.com/help/sidebar-navigation)); that page is **still unverified**, because it now redirects to a blocked host. The pane claim is **confirmed** from Workflowy's changelog, which also shows panes are new, shipped in August 2026: "Each pane is a view of an existing node, not a duplicate. Any edits you make in a pane update the original everywhere it appears" ([Workflowy What's new](https://workflowy.com/whats-new/)). The earlier detail that each pane has its own breadcrumbs and history could not be rechecked. Org-mode's narrowing and indirect buffers ([Org manual](https://www.gnu.org/software/emacs/manual/html_node/org/Global-and-local-cycling.html)) are still unverified, and Heptabase's sub-whiteboards and planned "sub-whiteboard preview mode" ([Heptabase roadmap](https://wiki.heptabase.com/roadmap)) were not rechecked.

The Muse entry needs **correcting**. The earlier report cited a Setapp listing; the product is now Allume, renamed in version 4.0 on 11 June 2026, still run by one developer and "building for individual thinkers over teams" ([Allume updates](https://allume.com/updates); [Allume memo](https://allume.com/memos/2026-07-allume-v4/)). It nests boards without limit, returns to a parent by pinching out or through a breadcrumb whose long trails collapse into an ellipsis menu, and describes its data as "Local-first data with instant sync" ([Allume](https://allume.com/)). That gives a primary-source confirmation of the breadcrumb-ellipsis device the earlier report could only attribute to Workflowy.

More important, the claim that **"every sourced tool zooms into existing content; none opens a blank page" no longer holds as worded.** The 2019 Muse prototype opened a blank child workspace inside the current one: "we achieve this instant-start by dragging from the edge of the screen to create a new nested board", with the stated benchmark of "turning to a new page in a paper notebook" ([Ink & Switch: Muse](https://www.inkandswitch.com/muse/)). The blank board is a new card, not a drill into an existing passage, and it carries no goal line or excerpt of its parent. So blank child workspaces exist on canvases. What no source read does is bind that blank space to a specific span of a parent text and hold the span as the prompt. The Zettelkasten practice of treating fleeting notes only as prompts for fresh writing ([Zettelkasten Forum](https://forum.zettelkasten.de/discussion/comment/7509)) still supports the choice and was not rechecked.

Matuschak's notes, now read first-hand, supply rationale more than prior art. He describes the blank page as the problem with naive writing ("there's nothing external, so you must juggle all of the piece-to-be in your head") ([Matuschak](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/zCknixwETdFm1MWdWPwMcXs)), which is an argument for ThinkPad's sub-pad always carrying its source span. He also argues that giving tangents somewhere to go "can free the author to focus on their 'primary' line of thinking" ([Matuschak](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/zDGk8tvVDD1mbTgpMMB5UGh)). His own order is the reverse of ThinkPad's: small notes first, concatenated into a manuscript, then rewritten. The earlier attribution of stacked panes to him is **still unverified in his own words**; his site implements a stack but no note read explains or argues for it.

The disorientation worry (Conklin 1987, not rechecked: [Conklin](https://www.ics.uci.edu/~andre/informatics223s2017/conklin.pdf)) gains a primary-source data point. Upwelling's authors tried drafts that depended on other drafts and "found that it made the model more difficult for users to understand and did not add meaningful value" ([Upwelling](https://www.inkandswitch.com/upwelling/)); Patchwork allows "no branching from branches", while admitting "occasional cases where it's not enough" ([Patchwork notebook](https://www.inkandswitch.com/patchwork/notebook/2024-version-control/)). Both findings come from multi-author use, but they cut against the earlier report's judgement that a strict tree is the benign case.

### Resolve and fold-back

The claim that every mechanism relating child content to a parent keeps the two identical or makes a one-off copy is **confirmed for the sources that could be read** and contradicted by none. Workflowy panes are live views and its changelog treats mirrors as copies of the same node ([Workflowy What's new](https://workflowy.com/whats-new/)). Allume's linked cards "are identical and tied together... there's no 'original' card" ([Allume memo](https://allume.com/memos/2022-09-linked-cards/)). Workflowy's mirror help page ([Workflowy mirrors](https://workflowy.com/learn/mirrors)), Obsidian's embeds and Tana Outliner's references are still unverified; Notion synced blocks ([Notion Academy](https://notion.com/help/notion-academy/lesson/synced-blocks)), Logseq embeds ([Logseq forum](https://discuss.logseq.com/t/the-difference-between-logseqs-block-embeds-and-block-references/8459)) and Roam's "replace with text" ([Outliner Software forum](https://www.outlinersoftware.com/topics/viewt/9127)) were not rechecked.

The primary sources add three near-precedents the earlier report lacked. Patchwork merges a whole-document branch back to main, then deletes the branch: "When you merge the branch, it's deleted. There's no cleanup step" ([Patchwork notebook](https://www.inkandswitch.com/patchwork/notebook/2024-version-control/)). An editor interviewed for Upwelling describes the folk version of resolve: "sometimes you make a copy of the doc and work in the copy, then paste it back" ([Upwelling](https://www.inkandswitch.com/upwelling/)). Matuschak's outline method substitutes whole notes for outline entries unchanged ([Matuschak](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/z79rMNNuLrUj6R8q1CXSpDm)). None is reductive: no source read describes a tool in which separate work, once condensed to one idea, replaces a passage while the working-out is archived. **The claim survives in narrowed form, with a workaround and a whole-document merge as its closest relatives.**

### Freeze, stage and version

The earlier sentence "no sourced tool freezes a draft and then restricts change to structured operations" is **corrected**. Upwelling, published in March 2023, builds a document from layers: "Changes to the document must be made on a draft. When the draft is ready, it is merged onto the stack", and "A merged layer can no longer be modified; to make any further edits, the writers must create a new draft." Drafts are titled "to describe their intent", accepted or deleted whole, and rebased so that "the stack forms a linear version history" ([Upwelling](https://www.inkandswitch.com/upwelling/)). That is frozen base text, staged changes, batch application and numbered linear versions, all ahead of ThinkPad. Upwelling is a research prototype and says plainly that it "is designed for multi-author collaboration, not for individual writing." What still holds of the old claim is the second half: inside an Upwelling draft, "a writer can edit the document as they would in any other text editor", and no source read restricts revision to a fixed vocabulary of moves.

A second partial precedent comes from an unexpected direction. Tana's current document product makes every AI change "a proposal you review", limited to three operations (create, update shown as a diff, delete), with "Accept All", a version picker and read-only older versions ([Tana: Proposals and actions](https://tana.inc/learn/features/proposals)). The restriction applies to the AI, while humans edit freely, so it does not contradict the narrowed claim. It does show that staged, structured, batch-applied revision is shipping in a mainstream tool. The earlier report missed that tana.inc now documents this separate product, with the outliner rebranded "Tana Outliner" ([Tana guide](https://tana.inc/learn/guides/connect-tana-outliner)).

Patchwork then supplies the strongest criticism of the design ThinkPad has chosen. Its authors judged Upwelling too formal for "requiring authors to create an explicit 'draft layer' before making any edits", adopted "formality on demand: choose the level of structure you want, and avoid needless ceremony", let users create a branch retroactively from the current edit session, and found that "grouping edits into atomically revertable units isn't a common need" for prose ([Patchwork notebook](https://www.inkandswitch.com/patchwork/notebook/2024-version-control/)). They also report the branch model "compelling for individual use" and that merging a batch as one history item "makes a batch of changes feel more satisfying to finish". ThinkPad's one-way freeze is therefore a deliberate bet against a published finding, defensible on the ground that single-user thinking has different needs from collaboration, but it should be presented as a bet.

The rest of the earlier picture is carried forward without recheck: The Most Dangerous Writing App's deletion penalty ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Most_Dangerous_Writing_App)), Ulysses' revision mode ([MacRumors forum](https://forums.macrumors.com/threads/ulysses-21-brings-advanced-grammar-and-style-check-to-iphone-and-ipad.2258041)), Scrivener snapshots with compare ([Well-Storied](https://www.well-storied.com/blog/how-to-review-old-drafts-with-scrivener-snapshots)) and the closure of Draft ([Beeminder help](https://help.beeminder.com/article/121-draft)). Patchwork corroborates Scrivener's "named snapshots" in passing, while calling such features "limited compared to the powerful capabilities that software engineers have".

### Tagging and the gate

Neither was rechecked directly. Tagging one's own paragraphs with a fixed vocabulary of intents was still not found; the progressive-summarization warnings ([Forte Labs](https://fortelabs.com/blog/progressive-summarization/); [Milo on Medium](https://medium.com/@nickmilo22/why-progressive-summarization-must-die-c2635d1f79f1)) stand as before. The gate's one software precedent, Org-mode's `org-enforce-todo-dependencies` with its `NOBLOCKING` exemption ([Org manual](https://www.gnu.org/software/emacs/manual/html_node/org/TODO-dependencies.html)), is **still unverified**, and Linear's parent auto-close ([Linear changelog](https://linear.app/changelog/2024-09-06-auto-close-parent-and-sub-issues)), Jira's automation ([Atlassian KB](https://support.atlassian.com/automation/kb/close-the-parent-issue-when-all-its-sub-tasks-are-closed/)) and the Rust RFC process ([RFC template](https://github.com/rust-lang/rfcs/blob/master/0000-template.md); [RFC 2561](https://rust-lang.github.io/rfcs/2561-future-possibilities.html)) were not reread. The mapping of Rust's handling of unresolved questions onto address, lift, park and caveat remains the best external argument that the four-way split is natural, and it remains an inference.

Two primary sources now bear on the gate as cautions. Matuschak writes that "Typical software systems are over-formal. They insist on finality", and that a paragraph moved to a "graveyard" section is one "you don't trust that you'll ever see... again" ([Matuschak](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/z4xE8FBHhDo5pTvJuCBPRYC)). The first is a warning about the freeze and the gate; the second says the archive behind a resolved span only works if it is trivially reachable from that span.

## The Focus runner: Intend starts blank and developer tools already push and pop

**Single-task execution is confirmed as list-fed in the mainstream.** Amazing Marvin's Super Focus Mode shows one task at a time in "the same [order] as... your daily list", and "the next task automatically appears" ([Marvin SFM](https://help.amazingmarvin.com/amazing-marvin/super-focus-mode-sfm)). Llama Life's own copy reads "I'm here to help you work THROUGH your list, ONE task at a time", with a countdown set from the estimate and the list's total duration and finishing time shown alongside ([llamalife.co bundle](https://llamalife.co/static/js/main.3b22df90.js)). Sunsama ([The Sweet Setup](https://thesweetsetup.com/some-first-impressions-of-sunsama-the-focus-course-in-app-form/)), Tiimo, Routinery and Super Productivity ([GitHub README](https://github.com/super-productivity/super-productivity)) were not rechecked, and Blitzit is unverified.

**The earlier claim that Mindstream is alone in being focus-first is contradicted.** Intend's Now Page "always starts blank, even if you've already set intentions for the day, and just asks 'What now?' You choose something to work on, by typing in an intention or picking one from various lists", and on completion "it doesn't automatically show you the next item on the list. It goes back to the expansive, open-ended 'What now?'" ([Intend features](https://intend.do/features)). Intend is otherwise flat: up to ten goals, intentions tagged with a goal number, no backlog ("You can't put in tasks for the future"), and hierarchy delegated to Workflowy through an unofficial integration ([Intend philosophy](https://intend.do/philosophy); [Intend FAQ](https://intend.do/faq)). The Now Page has no stack; finishing returns to a blank prompt, not to a parent.

**The claim that no manual push-and-return mechanic exists is also contradicted.** The earlier search missed a genre of small developer tools built for exactly this. yakstack: "Got distracted from a task? Add it to the yak stack... Once you've finished, shave the yak and go back to the previous task", printing the chain as an indented trail with "You are currently 2 yak frames deep" ([yakstack README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/gilmoreorless/yakstack/HEAD/README.rst)). memo calls itself "Your brain's task stack", shows elapsed time on the current item, and offers `pop` ("Complete the current task and resume the previous one"), `drop` (abandon and resume), `switch` and `queue` ([memo README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mattmanning/memo/HEAD/README.md)). rabbit_hole, stackly, todostack.el and sigi do the same, sigi with several named stacks ([rabbit_hole](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/davefp/rabbit_hole/master/README.md); [sigi](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/sigi-cli/sigi/HEAD/README.md)). yakstack also contradicts the claim that nothing shows the current task under its chain of parents. These are command-line and hobby projects whose maintenance status could not be checked, so the accurate statement is that **the stack exists as niche developer utilities and is absent from the mainstream task apps read.** Their framing differs from Mindstream's: they record being sidetracked, while Mindstream's push is deliberate decomposition. The mechanism is the same.

**"Decomposition in the moment is mostly AI" is refined.** It is true of Llama Life, whose "Break it down" posts the task to a server and returns generated steps with "Run again" and "Add to list", flattening them into the main list with no parent kept ([llamalife.co bundle](https://llamalife.co/static/js/main.3b22df90.js)). Marvin documents manual break-down as the purpose of sub-tasks ("break it down into smaller steps so it is easier to get started") and can replay them one at a time in focus mode ([Marvin subtasks](https://help.amazingmarvin.com/amazing-marvin/subtasks-checklist)), and Intend lists a "Break down intention into smaller steps" hotkey whose behaviour is undocumented ([Intend features](https://intend.do/features)). Marvin's is decomposition before execution, the reverse of Mindstream's order. Goblin Tools ([Gigazine](https://gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20250326-magic-todo)) and OmniFocus sequential projects ([OmniFocus manual](https://support.omnigroup.com/documentation/omnifocus/universal/4.10.1/en/perspectives/)) were not rechecked.

**The takeaway filed on the parent survives as the Focus runner's one unmatched element**, confirmed among the sources read with three near-misses. Intend, on finishing a goal's top priority: "you get a chance to reflect on what you learned. After that, it shows up on your timeline as a milestone", which is a completion note on the parent at one level only. Intend's daily outcomes ask per goal "is this enough?" with a "say more..." box ([Intend features](https://intend.do/features)). Taskwarrior accepts text at the moment of finishing, `task 1 done Paint dried overnight`, and stores it as an annotation on the finished task itself ([Taskwarrior done](https://taskwarrior.org/docs/commands/done/)). Marvin and Llama Life record time, points or confetti; memo's pop logs duration only. The earlier description of Intend's "fractal review system" is confirmed on the vendor's page and refined: its reviews synthesize "tiny notes about what's happening each day, up to the scale of weeks, months, seasons & whole years", which is roll-up by time within a fixed goal, a different axis from Mindstream's roll-up by decomposition depth. Session and Focusmate were not rechecked.

**Parking.** Taskwarrior's `wait:` semantics are confirmed: a date that hides the task until it passes, with no reason field, and a "context" that is only "a named filter" ([Taskwarrior dates](https://taskwarrior.org/docs/using_dates/); [Taskwarrior context](https://taskwarrior.org/docs/context/)). Marvin's dependencies are confirmed in detail: a blocked item moves to the Backburner showing "depends on...", and when the blocker completes it returns with a "new" tag whose hover "tells you why this item became available" ([Marvin dependencies](https://help.amazingmarvin.com/amazing-marvin/dependencies)). The first-pass researchers' belief in a dedicated Marvin "Waiting for" strategy is **contradicted**: waiting is a label convention, and a "waiting on" label can be wired to add a review date ten days out ([Marvin review date](https://help.amazingmarvin.com/amazing-marvin/review-date)). Because that label applies to projects as well as tasks, "all waiting features act on single tasks" is **refined**: Marvin can mark a whole body of work as waiting and resurface it by date, though with no free-text reason and no stack position to restore. The nearest design to Mindstream's park is Yak Stacker, a README-stage browser app in which stopping a task with its nested tasks invites a note under "What Do You Need To Remember Later?" ([yak-stacker README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/insin/yak-stacker/HEAD/README.md)). Parking a whole stack with a "waiting on" reason remains unmatched in the sources read. OmniFocus, FacileThings, Linear's blocked flag and Jira's flag were not rechecked.

**The research backing needs one correction.** The Ness Labs article describes interstitial journaling as writing "a few lines every time you take a break", credits Tony Stubblebine, and cites no study; it does not mention attention residue or Sophie Leroy ([Ness Labs](https://nesslabs.com/interstitial-journaling)). Any link between that practice and Leroy and Glomb's "ready-to-resume" finding ([UW News](https://www.washington.edu/news/2018/01/16/task-interrupted-a-plan-for-returning-helps-you-move-on/), not rechecked) is Mindstream's own inference. The closest support Ness Labs offers for a small completion ritual is Weigelt and Syrek's "closure" finding about unfinished tasks ([Ness Labs](https://nesslabs.com/unfinished-tasks)).

## Workflows were not rechecked, but the routing slip sets their limit

The workflows section of the earlier report stands as written and unverified. Ordered prompts producing one entry is a consumer pattern (Stoic's custom journals: [App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/stoic/id1312926037); Day One templates: [Day One guide](https://dayoneapp.com/guides/tips-and-tutorials/templates/)), with the CBT thought record as the closest structural precedent, "a set of 7 prompts" ending in one balanced thought ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/every-mind-matters/mental-wellbeing-tips/self-help-cbt-techniques/thought-record)). Tools for thought stop at "expand, then forget": QuickAdd cancels the whole run if one prompt is cancelled ([QuickAdd docs](https://quickadd.obsidian.guide/docs/ControllingPrompts/)), and Tana's template and command-node pages now sit on the blocked outliner host. Thinking methods ship as worksheets ([Untools](https://www.untools.co/templates/); [Farnam Street](https://fs.blog/2014/02/decision-journal/)); CFAR's concession that Double Crux often ends in a single crux ([LessWrong](https://www.lesswrong.com/lw/o6p/double_crux_a_strategy_for_resolving_disagreement/divl)) is still the best case for "a sharper question" as a legitimate ending; Fatebook's YES, NO or AMBIGUOUS ([LessWrong](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/yS3d46m23wRKDQobt/introducing-fatebook-the-fastest-way-to-make-and-track)) is still the model of a small closed outcome set. Shipman and Marshall's "Formality Considered Harmful" ([paper](https://people.engr.tamu.edu/shipman/formality-paper/harmful.html)) remains the governing warning.

The claim that no personal step-by-step runner captures text per step is **refined, and still largely unverified**, because routine and process-run products were outside this pass. One counter-example of a kind turned up: Marvin's Procrastination Wizard "walks you through a series of questions and tips", and "Some steps ask you to write something down or start a short timer" ([Marvin help](https://help.amazingmarvin.com/amazing-marvin/procrastination-wizard)). It is a single-user guided sequence with text capture, but fixed by the vendor, and the help page does not say what happens to the text. Intend's reviews are "pre-loaded with questions" and accept custom questions per goal ([Intend features](https://intend.do/features)), which is periodic, not procedural. Llama Life presets are reusable lists run one task at a time with no capture.

The most consequential new finding for workflows comes from the interop sources. A list of prompts that travels on the message is the Routing Slip pattern exactly: "Attach a Routing Slip to each message, specifying the sequence of processing steps" ([Routing Slip](https://www.enterpriseintegrationpatterns.com/patterns/messaging/RoutingTable.html)). Its authors state the limit: "the sequence of processing steps has to be determined up-front and the sequence is linear", and when "routing decisions might have to be made based on intermediate results" the answer is a Process Manager that holds state in the hub ([Process Manager](https://www.enterpriseintegrationpatterns.com/patterns/messaging/ProcessManager.html)). **A Mindstream workflow can stay an ordered list on a dumb broker only as long as it never branches on an answer.** Branching by the user choosing a different workflow keeps the broker dumb; branching by the system does not. Ink & Switch's Potluck adds support for keeping the payload loose, urging readers "to consider the benefits of freeform data formats and deferring structured formalism" ([Potluck](https://www.inkandswitch.com/potluck/)).

## The planned shell is a textbook messaging design, with one strong counter-position

The shell remains the area where Mindstream is least novel and best supported, and it is now also the best verified.

**Registry and commands: confirmed.** VS Code's contribution points are "static declarations that you make in the `package.json` Extension Manifest", extensions load lazily, and `when` clauses over host-set context keys control menu visibility ([Extension anatomy](https://code.visualstudio.com/api/get-started/extension-anatomy); [when clauses](https://code.visualstudio.com/api/references/when-clause-contexts)). Two gaps are filled. Commands are a request-and-return mechanism: `executeCommand<T>` returns a `Thenable<T>`, and "Commands may also return a result" ([Command guide](https://code.visualstudio.com/api/extension-guides/command)). Menu ordering uses `group@order`, with the `navigation` group always first, and the docs separate `when` (hide, "to prevent menus full of disabled items") from `enablement` (grey out) ([Contribution points](https://code.visualstudio.com/api/references/contribution-points)). JupyterLab has "a single command registry, keyed by string command IDs", with `isEnabled` and `isVisible` on each command ([JupyterLab extension points](https://jupyterlab.readthedocs.io/en/stable/extension/extension_points.html)). Obsidian, by contrast, is a precedent for layout only: its documented commands take no arguments and return nothing to a caller ([Obsidian developer docs source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/obsidianmd/obsidian-developer-docs/main/en/Plugins/User%20interface/Commands.md)). Raycast was not rechecked.

**FDC3: corrected to version 3.0.** The earlier report cited 2.2; the current standard is 3.0, adopted 26 October 2026 ([FDC3 abstract](https://fdc3.finos.org/docs/fdc3-standard)). The intent, result and two-stage error mechanics are confirmed unchanged, including `UserCancelled`, `TargetInstanceUnavailable`, `NoResultReturned` and the 15-second rule that covers only launch and listener registration, with no deadline for a result ([FDC3 errors](https://fdc3.finos.org/docs/api/ref/Errors); [API overview](https://fdc3.finos.org/docs/api/spec)). Version 3.0 adds what amounts to a written rule for a dumb broker. The Desktop Agent owns two envelope fields and ignores attempts to set them ("Apps SHOULD NOT provide `source`; if they do, the Desktop Agent MUST ignore it", and the same for timestamps); it "MUST generate a `traceId` when the originating application did not supply one, so that a `traceId` is always present"; and it must forward app-provided metadata unchanged, never inspecting, stripping or modifying signature fields. Payload and envelope are separate arguments. The resolver's look and ordering are explicitly not standardised, the chooser is skipped when resolution is unambiguous, and the spec names one-way hand-off ("Chain") as "the primary focus", which makes request-and-return the less-trodden path even there ([API overview](https://fdc3.finos.org/docs/api/spec)).

**The envelope is a standard combination.** The Enterprise Integration Patterns site defines each piece Mindstream planned: Request-Reply, where an asynchronous callback "must re-establish the caller's context" ([Request-Reply](https://www.enterpriseintegrationpatterns.com/patterns/messaging/RequestReply.html)); Return Address, "put in the header of a message because it's not part of the data being transmitted" ([Return Address](https://www.enterpriseintegrationpatterns.com/patterns/messaging/ReturnAddress.html)); Correlation Identifier ([Correlation Identifier](https://www.enterpriseintegrationpatterns.com/patterns/messaging/CorrelationIdentifier.html)); and Document Message, which "just passes data and lets the receiver decide what, if anything, to do", against Command Message ([Document Message](https://www.enterpriseintegrationpatterns.com/patterns/messaging/DocumentMessage.html)). SEND is a Document Message, ASK a Command Message with a reply, and two message shapes form a tiny Canonical Data Model. Only the site's summaries were read; the book's full text was not.

**x-callback-url: confirmed against the specification.** All four parameters are optional, `x-source` is "the friendly name of the source app", cancel is distinct from error, and the spec defines no timeout and no behaviour if the target never calls back ([x-callback-url spec](https://x-callback-url.com/specification/)). It has a return address but no correlation identifier, so Mindstream carrying both improves on it. The Drafts complaint about a callback that "hangs if app does not return" ([Drafts forum](https://forums.getdrafts.com/t/callbackurl-hangs-if-app-does-not-return/2633)) was not rechecked, but the spec's silence explains it.

**The plumber: corrected.** The earlier report listed the Plan 9 plumber beside "dumb pipes" as "a router with user-editable rules". Pike's paper shows it is a smart pipe: it "dispatches and reformats" messages, and rules "may modify the attribute list or replace the data section". It is also strictly one-way: "the plumber has no concept of a reply to a message", and non-blocking delivery was given "priority over reliability". Two of its mechanics transfer well: a message that matches no rule fails the sender's write immediately, and `plumb client` "keeps the message around for delivery when the application opens the port". Pike's own lesson from the first version was that "there was no way to discover whether a port was in use" ([Pike](https://9p.io/sys/doc/plumb.html)). Mindstream's broker is dumber than the plumber, and ASK has no precedent there.

**Dumb pipes: confirmed at source.** The earlier report cited Nordic APIs. The original is James Lewis and Martin Fowler, 25 March 2014: "smart endpoints and dumb pipes", against buses with "sophisticated facilities for message routing, choreography, transformation, and applying business rules" ([Lewis and Fowler](https://martinfowler.com/articles/microservices.html)). That list is a checklist of what the broker must never acquire. Fowler's Tolerant Reader adds the consumer's rule: "only take the elements you need, ignore anything you don't", and "make sure there's only one bit of code that reads data payloads" ([Fowler](https://martinfowler.com/bliki/TolerantReader.html)).

**Layout: confirmed and sharpened.** JupyterLab restores a pane by re-running a registered command with saved JSON arguments, offers `?reset` and `?clone`, and adds a rule the earlier report lacked: "A workspace should only be open in a single browser tab at a time", with a prompt for a new name on a clash ([JupyterLab URLs](https://jupyterlab.readthedocs.io/en/stable/user/urls.html); [JupyterLab workspaces](https://jupyterlab.readthedocs.io/en/stable/user/workspaces.html)). Obsidian's workspace is a tree of splits and tabs whose leaves are typed by view id, its docs warn "Never manage references to views", and leaves a plugin added "remain even after the plugin is disabled" ([Obsidian developer docs source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/obsidianmd/obsidian-developer-docs/main/en/Plugins/User%20interface/Workspace.md)); its Workspaces core plugin saves and switches layouts ([Obsidian help source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/obsidianmd/obsidian-help/master/en/Plugins/Workspaces.md)). The earlier researchers' notes were wrong on one Obsidian point that the earlier report did not repeat: core Obsidian URIs do accept `x-success` and `x-error` on some actions, and plugins can register `obsidian://` handlers officially ([Obsidian URI source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/obsidianmd/obsidian-help/master/en/Extending%20Obsidian/Obsidian%20URI.md)).

**The web platform offers no shortcut.** MDN marks Web Share as "Limited availability"; it hands off to operating-system targets, requires a user gesture and returns only success or failure. Web Share Target is experimental, works only for an installed app and arrives as a navigation ([MDN Web Share](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/Web_Share_API); [MDN share_target](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/Progressive_web_apps/Manifest/Reference/share_target)). Neither can carry a reply, so both belong at the outer edge of Mindstream, never between tools.

**Android, macOS Services and the shelf evidence were not rechecked.** Android's line between the Sharesheet and the intent resolver, its share-menu rules and its Activity Result design ([Android sharing](https://developer.android.com/training/sharing/send); [Android results](https://developer.android.com/training/basics/intents/result)) stand on the first pass, where they were among the few pages read in full. Dropover and Yoink remain thin evidence for a shelf. One better-sourced shelf precedent is new: Muse's "informal scratch space" that "can be summoned and dismissed with a swipe, and it moves with you as you zoom around the space", alongside excerpts that "know where they came from, and can take you back to that context" ([Ink & Switch: Muse](https://www.inkandswitch.com/muse/)).

**The strongest counter-position is shared data, and it is now quoted, not summarised.** Ink & Switch argue that "it's essential that those tools can operate on one shared reality", that "when each application manages its own data, introducing a new tool into a workflow incurs more friction", and that the missing ingredient is "a modern equivalent to the filesystem" ([Malleable Software](https://www.inkandswitch.com/essay/malleable-software/)). Embark names the cost of copies: "Context is not shared across apps" ([Embark](https://www.inkandswitch.com/embark/)). The interoperability sentence the earlier report had second-hand is confirmed verbatim. The essays do not set shared data against message passing as architectures; that contrast is this report's reading. Their own evidence also supports Mindstream's trade for one developer: the unsolved part of their approach is schema agreement ("Upgrading tools would frequently break compatibility with existing data"), and their answer, Cambria, is not production-ready. Mindstream takes the half of their programme it can afford, a shared workspace where tools are used together, and declines the half that is unsolved. A message carrying plain text and a prompt list has almost no schema to disagree about.

## How Mindstream should now describe what is distinctive

Three descriptions should be retired. Mindstream is not the first tool to freeze a draft and stage changes against it: Upwelling did that in 2023. It is not the first to offer a manual stack of what you are doing: at least six developer utilities do. It is not the first focus tool to start from a typed, blank prompt: Intend's Now Page does. Saying any of these invites a one-link rebuttal.

Three descriptions are defensible on the evidence read. **ThinkPad is a span-scoped, single-user, nested form of draft-and-merge**, with two additions no source has: the child starts blank instead of as a copy, and a convergence condition, one idea, gates the merge while the working-out is archived. **The Focus runner turns the programmer's task stack into a thinking tool**: decomposition is the push gesture, a takeaway is prompted at every pop and filed on the parent, and a whole stack can be parked with a reason. Of those elements, only the takeaway on the parent has no partial precedent. **The shell is deliberately unoriginal**: a routing slip, a return address and a correlation identifier over two message shapes, with a broker that stamps and forwards and never reads.

Acknowledging the lineage is an asset. Citing Upwelling and Patchwork places ThinkPad in a serious research line and lets Mindstream state its bet precisely: that for one person thinking alone, a mandatory freeze buys a clean separation of writing from judging, where Patchwork found mandatory drafts too ceremonious for collaborators. Citing yakstack and memo shows that the stack answers a need people keep building tools for. The distinctive claim that remains is the combination and the ordering rules, and that claim is exactly as strong as the unread sources allow.

## The risks, updated: ten instead of seven

**Cheap tagging can become a backlog.** Unchanged and not rechecked. Marking feels like progress and produces obligations; guilt-free "park" and "accept as caveat" and a live open count are the mitigations.

**A self-enforced gate and a mandatory freeze are a bet against published experience.** The earlier risk was that a solo user disables the gate or rubber-stamps "park". The primary sources widen it. Patchwork's authors abandoned mandatory drafts for "formality on demand", and Matuschak warns that software "insist[s] on finality". Both stages of ThinkPad that say "you may not" carry this risk, and neither precedent tested single-user thinking, so the outcome is open. Rust's detail that the strict check bites only at the final level still suggests making "lift" the easy default below the outermost conclusion.

**An archive nobody trusts defeats resolve.** New. Matuschak's writer will not move a paragraph to a graveyard because "you don't trust that you'll ever see it again". If the working-out behind a resolved span is more than one gesture away, users will hesitate to converge, or will stuff the working-out into the result.

**Deep nesting confuses.** New. Upwelling dropped dependent drafts because users found them harder to understand, and Patchwork allows one level. Drill and the focus stack both invite depth. A depth count and a soft discouragement are cheap; the evidence comes from multi-author settings and may not transfer.

**Structure can arrive too early.** Unchanged, with Potluck's call for "deferring structured formalism" as added primary support. Workflows should stay opt-in on existing text; nested workflows are the most exposed feature.

**Prompt sets wear out, fixed sequences coerce, and a linear slip will be pressed to branch.** The first two halves are unchanged and not rechecked, as is the warning that a personal prediction record stays too small to be informative ([LessWrong](https://greaterwrong.com/posts/Dxk4odvhTkHFZEwEZ/what-value-does-personal-prediction-tracking-have)). The third is new: the Routing Slip's authors say that the moment a next step depends on an intermediate result, state moves into the hub. The first conditional workflow is the point at which the dumb broker ends, and it should be refused or redesigned as a user choice.

**Send menus, callbacks and saved layouts each have a documented failure, and ASK is the less-trodden path.** Clutter in self-registering share menus ([Six Colors](https://sixcolors.com/?p=19105)), a hanging callback in Drafts and stuck JupyterLab workspaces were not rechecked. Confirmed additions: x-callback-url defines no timeout at all; FDC3 gives no deadline for a result and calls one-way hand-off its primary focus; the plumber refuses replies by design. Two layout hazards are new: two tabs writing one saved layout, and a saved layout that names a tool no longer present.

**The broker can grow, and copies lose context.** Lewis and Fowler's list (routing, choreography, transformation, business rules) names what creeps in. Embark names what copies cost: tools that "don't share the broader context of the task". Provenance in the envelope softens that without giving live linkage. If users come to want one text visible and current in two tools, Ink & Switch's shared-data argument becomes the better design, and message passing will have been the wrong foundation. Their unsolved schema problem is the reason not to start there. Obsidian's API changelog as evidence that any exposed surface becomes a burden ([changelog](https://unpkg.com/obsidian@1.13.1/CHANGELOG.md)) was not rechecked.

**The category is unstable.** The earlier product-status facts (Draft closed in 2022; Woebot, Centered, Arc and Logseq's split) were not rechecked. This pass adds movement of its own: Muse sold its name and continues as Allume under one developer, with no release listed after 30 June 2026; Tana split into two products and moved its outliner docs; Workflowy shipped panes only in August 2026; FDC3 moved to 3.0 a month before this report. Comparables go stale within a quarter.

**The novelty claims are fragile, and that is now demonstrated.** The earlier report warned that any "not found" could be overturned by one missed product or one blocked page. Two were overturned and one narrowed by the first direct read. The claims that remain rest on sources that were either unreadable (Workflowy help, Tana Outliner, Obsidian help, Org, Blitzit, Llama Life's apps) or never rechecked (Roam, Notion, Logseq, Heptabase, Sunsama, OmniFocus, the whole workflows strand). The mechanics with no precedent are still the ones with no evidence that users want them.

## Conclusion

The primary-source check moves Mindstream from "mechanics without peers" to "a new arrangement of known parts", and that is the stronger position. Each tool now has an identifiable ancestor with published findings attached: Upwelling and Patchwork for ThinkPad, the programmer's task stack and Intend for the Focus runner, the Enterprise Integration Patterns and FDC3 for the shell. Those ancestors come with test results Mindstream has not had to pay for. Mandatory drafts felt heavy to collaborators. Dependent drafts confused them. Merging a batch as one item felt satisfying. A routing slip cannot branch. A broker should stamp and forward and nothing else. The design questions worth spending time on are the ones those results leave open for a single thinker: whether the freeze earns its ceremony, whether one idea is the right convergence condition, and whether a takeaway prompted at every pop gets written or skipped.

The sequencing advice changes in one respect. The earlier report said to read the blocked primary sources before making any public novelty claim. That has now been done for about half of them, and it paid for itself immediately, so the other half should be read before the wording is fixed: Workflowy's help articles, Tana Outliner's docs, Obsidian's user help and the Org manual for ThinkPad; Blitzit and Llama Life's apps for the Focus runner; and at least one direct pass over the workflows strand, which is the only planned feature whose comparables no one has yet read at source.
